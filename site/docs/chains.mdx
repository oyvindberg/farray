  The introduction led with fusion, FArray's headline trick, which has{" "}
  <Link to="/fusion">its own section</Link>. This page is the plain library: no <code>.fuse</code>
  anywhere, just eager <code>FArray</code>, and it already beats the field. Single ops tie a raw

  String on the right.

The rivals throughout: Scala's `List` (an immutable linked list, not `java.util.List`), `Vector`
(a persistent tree), `IArray` (an immutable view of a raw array), and the array-backed `Chunk`
types from the fs2 and ZIO streaming libraries.

First the totals. Every size of every chained-operation benchmark class in the suite, eager FArray
only, no `.fuse`:

<ChainScore />

Three effects compound down a chain:

<ul className="points">
  <li>
    <strong>Boxing is paid per stage.</strong> A three-stage pipeline over boxed elements boxes at
    every crossing; chaining multiplies the tax rather than amortizing it. FArray's stages hand
    each other primitive arrays.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Structural stages cost nothing.</strong> A <code>take</code>, <code>drop</code> or{" "}
    <code>reverse</code> in the middle of a chain is an O(1) lazy node for FArray and a full copy for
    every array-backed rival, a copy of data whose only purpose is to be consumed by the next stage.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Big methods slow the rivals down.</strong> The array-backed collections run every lambda
    you pass to <code>map</code>/<code>filter</code> through one shared, generic copy of that method.
    Pack several different lambdas into one method and the JIT can no longer keep that shared copy
    specialized, so it deoptimizes and slows down. FArray compiles each lambda into its own loop, so
    there is nothing shared to spoil. This is what turns a one-line benchmark tie into a loss in real
    code, detailed on <Link to="/operations/map#where-it-stops-being-a-tie">the map page</Link>.
  </li>
</ul>

## flatMap → filter → take

`flatMap` doubles the sequence, `filter` thins it, and `take` throws a chunk of it away. FArray's
`take` is an O(1) SliceNode, so it discards the tail for *free* — everyone else copies out what they
just finished building. With flat-leaf `flatMap` feeding an unboxed `filter` on top, eager FArray
runs this shape up to **22× past List** and 6–10× past Vector and the Chunks, before a single
`.fuse`:

Slice stages are FArray's freebie: a lazy window over the source, no copy. A `map` then transforms
through the window, and the fold reads straight out of the result:

## A long interleaved structural chain

The last chain alternates `+:`, `:+`, `++` (prepend, append, concat) and
`reverse`, forced by one final `map`. For FArray that's a string of O(1) nodes and a single O(n)
traversal; for array-copying structures it's a full copy *per op*:

## No warmup lap

Every number above is steady-state, measured after the JIT has compiled the loop. FArray doesn't
need that lap. Its unboxing and monomorphic reads are in the bytecode from compile time, not
something the VM discovers at runtime, so it starts fast rather than warming up to fast. Measured
cold — single-shot, zero warmup, the JVM still interpreting — eager FArray is already ahead of the
whole field, before anything is compiled:

<BenchChart
  cls="ColdPipelineIntBenchmark"
  ignore={fused}
  caption="A long eager pipeline measured COLD: SingleShotTime, one uncompiled invocation at 100k elements (invocations/sec, higher is faster). Eager FArray leads before the JIT compiles anything: 1.7× past IArray, 3.4× past Vector, 4× past List, and 4–7× past the Chunks. The boxing collections gain little from warmup anyway — their cost is allocation, and the JIT does not eliminate a million Integers."
/>

---

There is one speed above this: handing the whole chain to the compiler.{" "}
<Link to="/fusion">Fusion</Link> takes these same pipelines and emits a single loop; the 14-stage
version runs ~15× past even FArray's own eager chains. The eager ops above are already the
strongest field; fusion is the ceiling.
