<p className="lede">
  Measured one op at a time (the op chapters later do exactly that), FArray ties a
  bare array and beats the boxing collections by whatever the boxing costs. But nobody writes one op.
  Real code chains them: transform, select, slice, aggregate. Chains are where the design
  compounds, because FArray's advantages <em>multiply per stage</em> while its costs don't. This page
  runs that argument on every pipeline twice, Int on the left and String on the right, so you can see
  which wins come from unboxing and which come from structure.
</p>

The rivals throughout: Scala's `List` (an immutable linked list, not `java.util.List`), `Vector`
(a persistent tree), `IArray` (an immutable view of a raw array), and the array-backed `Chunk`
types from the fs2 and ZIO streaming libraries.

First the totals. Every size of every chained-operation benchmark class in the suite, eager FArray
only, no `.fuse`:

Three effects compound down a chain:

<ul className="points">
  <li>
    <strong>Boxing is paid per stage.</strong> A three-stage pipeline over boxed elements boxes at
    every crossing; chaining multiplies the tax rather than amortizing it. FArray's stages hand
    each other primitive arrays.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Structural stages cost nothing.</strong> A <code>take</code>, <code>drop</code> or{" "}
    <code>reverse</code> in the middle of a chain is an O(1) lazy node for FArray and a full copy for
    every array-backed rival, a copy of data whose only purpose is to be consumed by the next stage.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Call sites stay private.</strong> Each FArray lambda binds to its own compiled loop at
    its own call site. Competitors funnel all lambdas through one shared <code>map</code>/
    <code>filter</code> site, which goes megamorphic as the method grows:{" "}
    <Link to="/operations/map">the mechanism</Link> that turns their microbenchmark ties into
    production losses.
  </li>
</ul>

## The classic: map → filter → fold

Transform, select, aggregate: the shape of half the collection code ever written. In
java.util.stream terms this is map → filter → reduce, with `foldLeft` being the reduce with a
seed. One boxed
crossing per stage for the field; `int[]` end to end for FArray:

On Int the boxing collections pay per stage (5.6× over Vector, 12× over List at 100k); IArray and zio.Chunk pay only the intermediate allocations. IntStream is the strongest rival here and the cell we report against ourselves: its runtime fusion builds no intermediates, so it beats our EAGER chain 1.5×, and .fuse recovers the lead at 1.1–1.2×. On String the same pattern, closer: Stream edges eager, fused leads."
/>

## Structure in the middle: take → drop → fold

Slice stages are FArray's freebie: a lazy window over the source, no copy. The fold then reads
straight through the window:

<BenchPair
  int="TakeDropFoldIntBenchmark"
  str="TakeDropFoldStrBenchmark"
  caption="take(n-1) → drop(1) → foldLeft. FArray's take/drop are O(1) SliceNodes; the fold traverses the window without either slice ever existing. The copiers pay for it: 2× over IArray (two full array copies), 7–15× over Vector, 34× over List at 10k+. One tie: zio.Chunk also slices lazily and folds within ~6–9% of us; the win here is over eager copying, not over everyone."
/>

## Structure at the end: filter → map → reverse

The mirror case: the structural stage last. FArray's `reverse` is a node pointing at the result;
everyone else allocates a third collection just to flip the second one:

## Expansion: flatMap → flatMap → map → fold

## The method-size effect: eight distinct maps

This chain exposes the shared-call-site problem: eight *different* lambdas through `map` in one
method, the profile any nontrivial method builds up:

<BenchPair
  int="MapMegaIntBenchmark"
  str="MapMegaStrBenchmark"
  caption="Eight distinct lambdas through map in one method. IArray's one shared Array.map site sees eight Function1 (Scala's lambda interface) classes, goes megamorphic, and re-boxes on Int: at 10k its boxed intermediates are still cache-resident and it even leads (our one 0.85× cell), but at 100k the re-boxing becomes memory traffic and it falls 4.2× behind (List 12×, Vector 7×). FArray has no shared site to poison. On String there's no re-boxing to pay and the field stays close: the mechanism is megamorphic boxing."
/>

## A long interleaved structural chain

The last chain alternates `+:`, `:+`, `++` (prepend, append, concat) and
`reverse`, forced by one final `map`. For FArray that's a string of O(1) nodes and a single O(n)
traversal; for array-copying structures it's a full copy *per op*:

---

There is one speed above this: handing the whole chain to the compiler.{" "}
<Link to="/fusion">Fusion</Link> takes these same pipelines and emits a single loop; the 14-stage
version runs ~15× past even FArray's own eager chains. The eager ops above are already the
strongest field; fusion is the ceiling.
