## The chain that used to collapse

The case for compiling the pipeline instead of relying on the JIT, preserved as a benchmark: this
exact shape once ran at 0.055× of IArray when a trailing structural call silently disabled escape
analysis on a spliced loop (<Link to="/operations/flatmap">the full story</Link>). Fused, that
failure mode cannot occur:

## The other JIT: Temurin/C2

Everything above was measured on GraalVM CE, and fusion splices whole pipelines into single
methods, exactly the shape where JIT inliners differ most. So the headline claims were re-run on
the JVM most production fleets deploy: Temurin 25 (HotSpot C2), same machine, one fork per trial.

| Temurin 25, C2 | eager FArray | fused FArray | best rival |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 14-stage pipeline, 100k | 2.97× rival | **37.7× rival** (12.7× over eager) | zio.Chunk |
| 14-stage pipeline, 1k | 2.90× rival | **22.6× rival** (7.8× over eager) | zio.Chunk |
| map→filter→fold Int, 100k | 0.49× rival | **1.09× rival** (2.2× over eager) | IntStream |

Two findings, both worth stating plainly. The 14-stage headline holds on C2: 37.7× against 36× on
Graal; the speed genuinely lives in the emitted bytecode. And the short chain is where C2 tells a
different story: HotSpot compiles `IntStream`'s three-stage sink chain extremely well in a small
method, well enough to run *ahead of eager FArray* (which pays two intermediate arrays) and within
9% of fused. Runtime fusion is a real competitor on its best JIT at its best method size; the gap
that never closes is the one the megamorphic and method-growth pages measure, and the 14-stage
column above.
