  </li>

That last point is a consequence of how `inline` works here. `mapImpl` is `inline`, so the dispatch
and your lambda are resolved and lifted at the call site; `mapLeafIntInt` is not, so the
one heavy loop is compiled a single time and shared. The price of crossing that hand-off is that the
lambda has to become a function value, hence the SAM. But it's a *specialized* SAM, so nothing boxes,
and it's one allocation per `map` call, not per element; the JIT inlines its `apply` into the shared
loop at runtime. In the bytecode it's a single `anonfun` lifted by one `invokedynamic`, emitted once,
not twice.

The version that splices your lambda straight into the `while` loop, with no SAM at all, is `.fuse`,
which is why [fusion](../fusion/index.mdx) is a separate gear. What the inline layer buys on
its own is narrower but still decisive. A generic `map[B](f: A => B)` erases `A` and `B`
to `Object`, takes a boxed `Function1`, and round-trips every element through `Integer`. FArray never
writes that method down. The specialization lives in the call site instead of the signature, which is
why it holds even as the surrounding method grows and the JIT's inlining budget runs out.

String. On its own, `map` is no rout:

## Where it stops being a tie

Measured against a bare array, those two charts are a wash: a few percent shy on
`Int`, a dead tie on `String`. That's the texture of a *micro*-benchmark: one operation over one
array, in isolation, where a lone `Array.map` (the one shared generic map Scala's standard library
bolts onto raw arrays) is a call the JIT already inlines fully and the
opaque wrapper plus the specialized SAM are pure overhead it doesn't carry. FArray isn't built to win
those by a percent. It's built for what happens once the benchmark stops being a micro-benchmark,
and it doesn't take much.

Write eight of that very same `map`, with eight *distinct* lambdas, in one method. IArray
funnels all of them into its one shared `Array.map`, and that call site now sees eight different
`Function1` classes, well past the JIT's ≥3-type megamorphic threshold, so it stops devirtualising:
every element pays a real virtual `apply`, and on `Int` it re-boxes through `Integer` on the way out.
The bigger the method, the more distinct lambdas crowd that one site. The same story applies
verbatim to `java.util.stream`: a hot shared `Stream.map` feeds every lambda class through one
Sink chain. FArray has no
shared site to poison: each lambda was already bound to its own specialized loop at compile time, so
eight maps are eight tight `int[]` loops, however many of them you write.

One sibling deserves a name here: `mapConserve`, the identity-preserving map used by compilers and
transformers (`if (f(x) eq x)` for every element, return `this`). FArray's version keeps
the reference-reuse semantics and the specialized loop, and returns the *original* array untouched
when nothing changed: no allocation at all on the no-op pass.

## No warmup lap

Because all of that specialization happened at *compile* time, there's nothing for the JIT to
discover. FArray's speed is already in the bytecode (unboxed, monomorphic, allocation-free), so it
never waits on the VM to devirtualize or escape-analyze its way there. Measured cold (single-shot,
zero warmup, the JVM still interpreting), the fused pipeline is already three-to-four times ahead of
List and IArray, before anything has been compiled at all:

There's no lap where a boxing collection is briefly in front: the same pipeline widens to its ~36×
steady state as the loop compiles. The boxing collections gain little from warmup, because their
cost is *allocation*, and the JIT does not eliminate a million `Integer`s.
