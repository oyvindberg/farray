## Ten years of waiting for the JVM

<p className="lede">
  For most of a decade I tried to write fast code in Scala, and spent most of it frustrated. The
  collections are a joy to write — and box every primitive into the bargain. The <code>while</code> loop
  that doesn't box is a joy to nobody. Everything in between was a promise with a version number.
</p>

So I waited. For specialization that actually held up. For miniboxing. For the Dotty linker. For
Valhalla — always, eventually, for Valhalla, and the `List<int>` it kept almost promising: generics
specialized all the way down to flat, unboxed storage. That's the half of Valhalla that still hasn't
shipped. Every release I'd read the bytecode, fight the inliner, and arrive at the same verdict: if it
has to be fast, write it in something else. Usually Java. A genuinely terrible state of affairs for a
language this good.

### A thousand lines of spite

Eventually spite won. The initial commit lands in January 2023, at three in the morning: one
thousand-line file, the whole `IndexedSeq` API hand-written as `inline` `while` loops over a single
flat `Array[AnyRef]`, casts on the way out, `System.arraycopy` wherever shapes lined up. It worked —
flat storage and straight-line loops beat the collection library, exactly as the profiler always said
they would.

But it was boxed. The readme of the time is honest about it: *"if you need specialized primitives this
is not (at least yet) the library for you."* And that "yet" was load-bearing — unboxing wasn't a
feature you could bolt on, it wanted a different animal entirely: storage specialized per element kind,
a core the inliner couldn't defeat, machinery to keep a user's lambda unboxed across a library
boundary. I kept picking at it, and the experiments sprawled across an old machine, most of them never
reaching the repo — whose only commit in the three years that followed bumps the version of the build
tool. The shape of the thing was clear the whole time. The evenings weren't there.

## And then — boom

In June 2026 I picked it back up, this time with Claude doing the heavy lifting. Twelve days and
nearly three hundred commits later, the roadmap I'd been quietly resequencing for a decade had stopped
being sequential. The milestones didn't arrive one by one; they arrived **on top of each other**:

<ul className="points">
  <li>
    <strong>A working FArray.</strong> An <code>FArray[Int]</code> that <em>is</em> a real{" "}
    <code>int[]</code>, carrying the entire <code>IndexedSeq</code> API — <code>map</code>,{" "}
    <code>filter</code>, <code>fold</code>, <code>sort</code> — without a single{" "}
    <code>java.lang.Integer</code> anywhere. The thing the decade was spent waiting for.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Working fusion.</strong> A whole chain of <code>map</code> / <code>filter</code> /{" "}
    <code>flatMap</code> / <code>zip</code> / <code>fold</code>, rewritten at compile time into one pass
    over the backing array, allocating nothing between stages. The part I'd given up on ever having —
    its design note is dated one day before the first three working phases.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>And a working Claude.</strong> The reason the other two exist. The redesign the prototype
    demanded — a generated Java core, per-kind specialization, a macro optimizer — was years of evenings
    I didn't have. It became twelve days, with the benchmark suite as referee on every commit.
  </li>
</ul>

Everything on this site follows from those three landing together. And they stack — unboxed elements
under structural laziness under fused pipelines — so the fairest way to show it is to stack them.
Fourteen stages: `flatMap`, `filter`, `map`, `zip`, `zipWithIndex`, `foldLeft`, twice over — the kind
of pipeline you'd write on any ordinary Tuesday and pay for on every element. Every collection in the
race runs the identical code; the FArray version differs by a single word: `.fuse`. Before you look at
the chart, pick the speedup you'd refuse to believe.

At a hundred thousand elements: roughly **36× the quickest competitor**, **83× List** — and, the
number that says the most, **15× the very same pipeline run eagerly on FArray itself**. Whatever
factor you picked, the green bar probably clears it; it stands so far above the field that everyone
else flattens to slivers. Hover them for the exact numbers.

The reason it runs away with it: `.fuse` refuses to build any of the work-in-progress. Run those
stages eagerly and each one allocates an array whose only purpose is to be consumed and thrown away by
the next. Fusion collapses the whole chain into a single pass:

<FusionDiagram />

`.fuse` is a macro, not a runtime call. At compile time it reads the entire chain off the typed syntax
tree and rewrites it into one `while` loop over the backing array — the intermediate collections, the
`Function1`s, the boxing: gone. And this isn't a claim to take on faith. The lowering is checked into
the repo as a golden test, regenerated on every build. Here is exactly what those fourteen stages
compiled to.

That the Scala 3 macro system can do this — read a call chain off the tree, work out which columns
nobody downstream reads, and emit the specialized loop you'd have written by hand — is, frankly, a
little phenomenal. There's a whole section building [the fusion engine up from first principles](./fusion/index.mdx).

## The ladder

Now calibrate that number, because on its own it proves too little. Fast code on the JVM has never
been the hard part: an `int[]` and a `while` loop hit the memory-bandwidth ceiling on any JIT you can
buy. If you're willing to mutate in place, you can have that today, and nothing on this page beats
it. The hard part — the decade-old part — is getting within reach of that ceiling from **immutable,
expression-oriented collection code**: the style where values are values, erasure boxes your
primitives, every combinator is a call the inliner may abandon, and each stage of a pipeline
materializes a collection whose entire lifetime is one line.

So the honest way to read the chart is as a **ladder** — the same fourteen immutable stages on every
rung, and only the collection changes underneath them:

<Ladder />

**The bottom rungs** are the collections you already use. `List` and `Vector` pay per element, per
stage: a box, an allocation, a dispatch. The Chunks put arrays under the boxes and climb a little.
`IArray` drops the boxes and climbs further — but it's copying eagerly at every one of the thirteen
seams, and that's where it stops. Escape analysis will not save a fourteen-stage pipeline; the
intermediates are real.

**Rung five is the library.** Change the type, keep the code. FArray's storage is a genuine `int[]`
behind an opaque type, so there is nothing to box. Every lambda-taking operation is `inline`, so your
lambda compiles into the library's loop with the unboxing fixed in the method signature — it can't
quietly collapse back to boxing as the surrounding method grows, which is precisely where
competitors' "effectively unboxed" folds give out. And structural steps are O(1) nodes instead of
copies. No fusion yet — this rung is just a better collection, and it already clears everything below
it. [The next page](./chains.mdx) walks the evidence across every chained-operation benchmark in the
suite, and keeps its score live from the checked-in data.

**The top rung costs one word.** Iterators, views and LazyList have promised chain-collapsing for
years and delivered interpreter overhead. `.fuse` is not a runtime abstraction — it rewrites the
chain at compile time into the loop above, and the 15× it adds over our own eager rung is simply what
deleting thirteen materializations buys. The ladder tops out at the hand-written `while` loop,
because `.fuse` *emits* the hand-written while loop. You just never have to see it — or maintain it.

Most codebases will live on rung five — a type swap — and reach for the top rung where it matters.
The pages that follow are the climb in that order: first the chains rung five wins on its own, then
the machinery underneath — the Java core the loops run over, the Scala that keeps them unboxed, and
the cost model that makes it hang together.
