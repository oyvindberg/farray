<p className="lede">
  The front page says Claude did the heavy lifting, and it did: most of the code in this repository
  was written by a model. That sentence should raise questions, not settle them. This page is the
  answer to the right ones: who designed what, what the control system was, where the model was
  confidently wrong, and what the tests actually prove.
</p>

## The division of labor

**Designed by a human, rebuildable from memory:** the hybrid representation (flat primitive leaves
under lazy structural nodes), the decision to put the core in generated Java, the opaque-type
surface, the cost model the site keeps repeating (unboxing in signatures, not in JIT budgets), the
fusion contract (unsupported means does not compile), and every acceptance decision on every
benchmark number. Direction and taste stayed human throughout.

**Written by the model, reviewed by a human:** nearly all implementation code: the code generator,
the generated operations, the fusion macro and its optimizer, the benchmark suite itself. Review
here means read, questioned, and frequently sent back, not line-by-line formal verification. It
would be dishonest to claim otherwise about four hundred commits in seventeen days.

**Gated by machines:** every operation is tested against the equivalent `List` operation across
sizes and structural shapes (~480 parity tests). Every macro lowering is a golden file, regenerated
and diffed on every build. Every performance claim is a checked-in JMH JSON the site renders
directly. Nothing lands on the strength of an explanation.

## Where the model was wrong

The reason the referee exists. Three examples from the log, kept because they are instructive:

- **The dispatch theory.** The model (and, honestly, the human) believed the sealed-hierarchy
  `instanceof` dispatch in the node walk was a cost worth removing, and built an int-tag
  `tableswitch` version. Measured: 17 to 20 percent *slower* for monomorphic reads. HotSpot folds
  the sealed match better than the clever version. Reverted; the finding is now a comment that
  stops anyone from rebuilding it.
- **The refactor that did nothing.** A short-circuit scan gap was attributed to virtual dispatch
  and refactored away with conviction. The gap didn't move; it was the per-element loop shape all
  along. The commit was reverted and the loop-shape rule is now generator policy.
- **The fix that broke something else.** A deferred allocation in the fusion sink fixed a size-1
  cell and, two days later, the scorecard showed `groupMin` at 1000 elements running at half its
  previous speed. Bisecting three commits under measurement pinned it; the fix was re-gated. The
  model introduced the regression and also found it, but only because the whole suite is re-run and
  the JSON is diffed.

Several more live in the git log with their numbers. The pattern is consistent: plausible
mechanical reasoning about the JVM is wrong often enough that no claim survives here without a
measurement, whoever or whatever proposed it.

## What the tests prove, and what they don't

The parity suite samples behavior; it does not prove the optimizer's rewrite rules. Sinking a
computation past a filter or eliminating a dead tuple column is a semantic claim that golden tests
pin for the shapes we thought of. A shape nobody thought of can be wrong in a way nothing catches
until you hit it. That is the boundary, it is why the version number starts with M, and it
is why [Use it](./use.mdx) asks you to file the cliff instead of assuming we already know.
